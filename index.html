The Constant
Pain Theory

Everyone carries a full weight. The rich suffer in gold, the poor suffer in hunger — but the heaviness is the same.

Nafi May 2026 10 min read
"Pain is not distributed unequally across human lives — it is only translated into different languages."
Part I

The Happiness We're Chasing

We live in a world that constantly tells us where happiness lives. It lives in the next promotion, the bigger apartment, the newer phone, the better relationship. Society sells us a map — and most of us follow it without question. And when we finally arrive at where the map promised happiness would be, we look around and wonder why the feeling is already fading.

The reason is as old as human psychology itself: we adapt. Quickly, silently, and without our permission.

Psychologists call this the Hedonic Treadmill — the phenomenon where no matter what happens to us, good or bad, we eventually return to a baseline level of emotional wellbeing. We get the raise, we're thrilled for a month, and then it becomes the new normal. We face a loss, we grieve deeply, and slowly we rebuild. The treadmill keeps running. We keep walking. But we never actually move.

The problem isn't that we want happiness. The problem is that we keep looking for it in places where it was never designed to live permanently.

There are two fundamentally different kinds of happiness, and understanding the difference changes everything.

Type External Happiness Internal Happiness
Source Getting things, achieving status, external validation Perspective, gratitude, presence, meaning
Duration Short-lived — fades as we adapt Sustainable — doesn't expire
Effect over time Raises your baseline — you need more next time Stabilizes your baseline — you need less
Who has it Anyone who gets lucky or works hard enough Anyone who develops the skill

The person who finds deep joy in a single cup of tea, a warm conversation, or a quiet morning is not "easily pleased." They have developed a skill — the most underrated skill in existence: the ability to access happiness from the inside, rather than waiting for it to arrive from the outside.

Part II

The Theory: Pain Does Not Discriminate

Now here is where the original idea begins. Most of us, when we look at someone living in a mansion or driving a luxury car or travelling the world, feel a familiar ache: "Their life is so easy. Mine is full of problems." This comparison feels natural. It also happens to be deeply, fundamentally wrong — not morally wrong, but logically wrong.

Here is the core proposition of the Constant Pain Theory:

Core Statement

The total amount of pain a person experiences in life tends to be roughly equal across all humans, regardless of their wealth, status, or external circumstances. Pain is not an absolute measurement — it is always calculated relative to one's own personal baseline. Therefore, comparing your suffering to another person's is not just unhelpful — it is logically invalid.

This is a bold claim. Let us unpack exactly why it holds.

· · ·
1

Pain Is Relative to Your Baseline

Your brain does not measure pain on a universal scale. It measures deviation from what you are used to. If you eat every single day of your life and you miss one meal, that absence registers as real, genuine suffering. Meanwhile, someone who has known hunger for days may not register the same skipped meal as pain at all. Neither is wrong. Neither is exaggerating. Their nervous systems are simply calibrated to different normals. This is why your pain is always real — and why it cannot be compared to mine.

2

Pain Fills the Container

Think of every human life as a container of the same size. That container always fills up — with whatever problems your particular life provides. A wealthy person's container fills with fear of losing status, loneliness at the top, anxiety about trust, and the quiet emptiness of having achieved everything they chased. A struggling person's container fills with hunger, uncertainty, and injustice. Different water. Same container. Same weight. The problems simply upgrade themselves to match the life.

3

Joy, Too, Is Relative

The same logic applies to happiness. A small candy brings a street child a burst of joy that no luxury item could replicate for someone accustomed to fine things. The wealthy person needs an extraordinary experience to feel what the child feels from something simple. Neither is richer in joy — they are just measuring against different baselines. This means that the ability to feel joy is not bought. It is calibrated.

Part III

What Science Says

This theory did not emerge from nowhere. A remarkable body of psychological research quietly confirms its foundations — often in ways that surprised even the researchers themselves.

The Lottery Winners & Paraplegics Study

In one of psychology's most cited studies, researchers compared three groups: people who had recently won major lottery prizes, people who had become paraplegic due to accidents, and an ordinary control group. The expectation was obvious — lottery winners should be far happier, accident victims far less so. The result was extraordinary: after approximately one year, lottery winners reported no significantly higher happiness than the control group, and accident victims reported no significantly lower happiness either. Both groups had returned to near their original emotional baseline. The highs and the lows, in time, equalize.

Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman (1978) — Journal of Personality and Social Psychology

The Hedonic Treadmill

Psychologists Philip Brickman and Donald T. Campbell proposed in 1971 that humans are biologically wired to adapt to any new circumstance — positive or negative — and return to a stable emotional baseline. This means that the promotion, the new house, the dream holiday will bring happiness, but only temporarily. The brain quickly recalibrates what "normal" means, and the same level of happiness that the new thing produced becomes the baseline against which future experiences are measured. We are, in essence, running on a treadmill that always returns us to the same spot.

Brickman & Campbell (1971) — "Hedonic Relativism and Planning the Good Society"

The Easterlin Paradox

Economist Richard Easterlin discovered something deeply counterintuitive: within a given country, wealthier people tend to be slightly happier than poorer ones. But when entire nations get richer together, average happiness does not increase. Furthermore, comparing countries, rich nations are not significantly happier than poor ones. The conclusion? Happiness is relative. It is about where you stand in relation to your own reference point — not your absolute condition. Getting richer only helps if you are getting richer than the people around you. Once everyone rises together, the happiness effect vanishes.

Easterlin (1974) — "Does Economic Growth Improve the Human Lot?"

The Relative Deprivation Study

During World War II, sociologist Samuel Stouffer studied satisfaction among American soldiers. Remarkably, soldiers in units with high promotion rates were more dissatisfied than soldiers in units with low promotion rates. Why? Because those with more promotions around them had more people to compare themselves to — and always found someone ahead. Objective conditions mattered less than relative position. The theory of Relative Deprivation was born: we do not suffer from what we lack in absolute terms; we suffer from what we lack relative to our reference group.

Stouffer et al. (1949) — "The American Soldier: Adjustment During Army Life"

The Income & Wellbeing Ceiling

Nobel Prize-winning psychologist Daniel Kahneman and economist Angus Deaton found that beyond a certain income threshold — approximately $75,000 per year (at the time of the study) — more money stopped improving day-to-day emotional wellbeing. Basic comfort, security, and freedom from constant financial stress do contribute to happiness. But above that threshold, the daily emotional texture of a billionaire's life is not richer than that of a middle-class teacher. The problems simply change their costume.

Kahneman & Deaton (2010) — Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences
Part IV

Real Life, Same Story

Abstract theory becomes real when you see it in everyday life. Here are examples that most of us have either lived or witnessed — proof that the constant pain theory is not a philosophical idea but a lived reality.

    The Situation What the Theory Reveals
  • A child in poverty receives a new pencil and is overjoyed. A wealthy child gets the latest smartphone and shrugs within a week.
    Same object category — completely different joy threshold. The pencil is worth more to the child who has never had one.
  • A celebrated celebrity with millions in the bank dies by suicide. A street musician with nothing laughs every single day.
    Wealth does not equal an absence of suffering. The container fills regardless of what fills it.
  • A top student is devastated by a B+ grade. A first-generation university student would consider the same grade a triumph.
    Pain and joy are always measured against personal history — never against an objective standard.
  • A frequent business class traveller feels genuinely uncomfortable in economy. Someone flying for the first time finds economy magical.
    The baseline shifts. What was once luxury becomes the new normal, and what was once the normal becomes suffering.
  • World Happiness surveys regularly show that people in war-torn or extremely poor countries self-report life satisfaction levels that are surprisingly close to those in wealthy nations.
    The human capacity to adapt and find meaning equalises suffering across wildly different material conditions.
Part V

The Comparison Trap

Social psychologist Leon Festinger proposed in 1954 that humans are hardwired to evaluate themselves by comparing to others — it is called Social Comparison Theory. And it is, in many ways, our greatest psychological trap. We scroll through curated highlight reels and feel the sting of lives that look easier, richer, freer. We compare our behind-the-scenes to everyone else's best performance.

The Constant Pain Theory offers something more powerful than the usual advice to "just be grateful." It offers a logical argument:

The comparison is mathematically invalid to begin with. You are comparing your internal experience to their external appearance — and these two things were never on the same scale.

When you say "his life is so easy and mine is full of pain" — you are not making an observation. You are making an error. You are seeing their container from the outside, polished and presentable. You cannot see what is inside it. And if the theory holds, what is inside is just as heavy as yours — only shaped differently.

This does not mean all lives are equally hard in every moment, or that privilege and systemic suffering do not exist. They do. The theory acknowledges exceptions at the extremes — prolonged trauma, genocide, chronic illness — where the weight genuinely exceeds what adaptation can absorb. These are real. But they are the exception, not the rule of everyday life comparisons we make.

Nafi's Constant Pain Theory — Full Statement

Pain is not distributed unequally across human lives — it is only translated into different languages. Every person carries a full weight. The rich suffer in gold, the poor suffer in hunger, but the heaviness is the same. Because pain is personal and relative to one's own baseline, comparing your suffering to another's is not just unhelpful — it is logically invalid. And since happiness, too, is internal and always available, the only real variable in life is not how much pain you carry, but how skillfully you find joy within it.

Part VI

So What Do We Do With This?

If pain is constant and unavoidable — if the container always fills — then the goal of life cannot be to escape pain. That game is unwinnable. The goal shifts to something more honest and more achievable: developing the internal skill of finding happiness within the same amount of pain that everyone else also has.

This means a few things practically:

Stop the upward comparison. Not because you should suppress ambition, but because the comparison is built on false data. The person you envy is carrying their own full container — you just cannot see inside it.

Protect your baseline. Every luxury you normalise raises your baseline. The more you need to feel neutral, the more pain you will feel when it is absent. This is not an argument against enjoying good things — it is an argument for enjoying them as gifts, not as requirements.

Validate your own pain. Because pain is relative, a "small" problem is real pain if it deviates from your normal. You do not need to compare your suffering to someone else's worse suffering to give yourself permission to feel it. Your pain is valid by definition.

And extend the same understanding to others. The person whose "small" problem seems trivial to you is suffering genuinely within their own frame. The theory calls for empathy in both directions — inward and outward.

· · ·

Life is not a competition of suffering. It is a shared condition — one that every human, across every century and every continent, has navigated with the same basic equipment: a mind that adapts, a heart that aches, and the persistent, beautiful, stubborn capacity to find meaning anyway.

We are all, in the end, carrying the same weight. We just carry it in different shapes.
